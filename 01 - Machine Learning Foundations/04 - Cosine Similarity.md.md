# 04 - Cosine Similarity

## Introduction

In the previous chapter, we learned about the **dot product** and how it tells us whether two vectors point in similar directions.

The dot product is one of the most important operations in linear algebra. It appears everywhere in Machine Learning, Deep Learning, and Generative AI.

However, there is a major problem.

The dot product depends not only on the direction of the vectors but also on their lengths (magnitudes).

In many AI applications, we don't actually care how long a vector is.

We care about **how similar the meanings are**.

This is exactly why **Cosine Similarity** exists.

It compares vectors based only on their **direction**, completely ignoring their length.

This single idea powers semantic search, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), vector databases, recommendation systems, document search, and modern Large Language Models.

---

## Why Dot Product Is Not Enough

Suppose we have two vectors.

$$
A = [1,1]
$$

$$
B = [2,2]
$$

The dot product is

$$
A \cdot B = 1 \times 2 + 1 \times 2 = 4
$$

Now consider another vector.

$$
C=[100,100]
$$

The dot product becomes

$$
A \cdot C = 1\times100 + 1\times100 = 200
$$

Did the direction change?

No.

Vector **B** and **C** point in exactly the same direction.

Only their lengths changed.

Yet the dot product increased from **4** to **200**.

This shows the limitation of the dot product.

It cannot distinguish between

- vectors pointing in similar directions
- vectors simply having large magnitudes

For semantic search, this is a problem.

Imagine two sentence embeddings.

Sentence A:

> "Cats are wonderful pets."

Sentence B:

> "Cats make great companions."

If one embedding happens to have a larger magnitude because of the model's internal computations, we don't want it to appear **more similar** simply because its numbers are larger.

We only care about whether both embeddings point in nearly the same direction in vector space.

---

## The Idea Behind Cosine Similarity

Imagine placing two arrows starting from the origin.

If they point in exactly the same direction,

the angle between them is

$$
0^\circ
$$

If they are completely unrelated,

the angle is around

$$
90^\circ
$$

If they point in opposite directions,

the angle becomes

$$
180^\circ
$$

Cosine similarity measures the **cosine of this angle**.

Instead of comparing vector lengths,

it compares **orientation**.

This makes it an excellent measure of semantic similarity.

---

## Formula

The cosine similarity between two vectors is

$$
\boxed{
\text{Cosine Similarity}=
\frac{A\cdot B}
{||A||\,||B||}
}
$$

where

- $A \cdot B$ is the dot product
- $||A||$ is the magnitude of vector A
- $||B||$ is the magnitude of vector B

Recall that the magnitude of a vector is

$$
||A||=
\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots+x_n^2}
$$

Notice something interesting.

The numerator is the **dot product**.

The denominator removes the effect of vector length.

That is why cosine similarity focuses only on direction.

---

## Understanding the Output

Cosine similarity always lies between

$$
-1
$$

and

$$
1
$$

| Cosine Similarity | Meaning |
|------------------:|---------|
| 1 | Exactly the same direction |
| 0 | Perpendicular (no similarity) |
| -1 | Opposite directions |

For embeddings generated by language models,

values are usually between **0** and **1** because embeddings rarely point in exactly opposite directions.

Higher values indicate greater semantic similarity.

---

## Manual Calculation

Let

$$
A=[1,2]
$$

$$
B=[2,3]
$$

### Step 1 — Dot Product

$$
A \cdot B = 1\times2+2\times3 = 8
$$

### Step 2 — Magnitude of A

$$
||A|| = \sqrt{1^2+2^2} = \sqrt{5}
$$

### Step 3 — Magnitude of B

$$
||B|| = \sqrt{2^2+3^2} = \sqrt{13}
$$

### Step 4 — Cosine Similarity

$$
\frac{8}{\sqrt{5}\sqrt{13}} = \frac{8}{\sqrt{65}} \approx 0.9923
$$

The vectors are almost pointing in the same direction.

Therefore, their cosine similarity is very close to **1**.
---

## NumPy Implementation

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([1, 2])
B = np.array([2, 3])

similarity = np.dot(A, B) / (
    np.linalg.norm(A) * np.linalg.norm(B)
)

print(similarity)
```

Output

```text
0.9922778767
```

This is the same value we calculated manually.

---

## Building `cosine_similarity()` From Scratch

Instead of relying on NumPy's built-in functions, let's implement it ourselves.

```python
import math

def cosine_similarity(A, B):
    dot = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(A, B))

    magA = math.sqrt(sum(a * a for a in A))
    magB = math.sqrt(sum(b * b for b in B))

    return dot / (magA * magB)

A = [1, 2]
B = [2, 3]

print(cosine_similarity(A, B))
```

Output

```text
0.9922778767
```

This implementation works for vectors of any dimension.

## Real-World Sentence Embedding Example

Suppose an embedding model converts sentences into vectors.

| Sentence | Embedding (simplified) |
|----------|------------------------|
| Cats are amazing pets | [0.52, 0.14, 0.73, 0.20] |
| Cats make wonderful companions | [0.50, 0.12, 0.70, 0.18] |
| I love eating pizza | [0.10, 0.82, 0.04, 0.95] |

If we calculate cosine similarity,

```
Cats ↔ Cats
```

we may get

```text
0.98
```

Very similar.

For

```
Cats ↔ Pizza
```

the similarity might be

```text
0.18
```

Very different.

Notice that we never compare the actual words.

We compare their **embeddings**.

The embedding model has already converted meaning into vectors.

Cosine similarity simply measures how closely those vectors point in the same direction.

---

## Why Vector Databases Use Cosine Similarity

Modern vector databases store millions or even billions of embeddings.

Examples include

- FAISS
- ChromaDB
- Pinecone
- Milvus
- Weaviate

When a user asks a question,

the query is converted into an embedding.

The database then computes the similarity between the query embedding and every stored embedding.

The documents with the highest cosine similarity are returned first.

For example,

Query:

> "How do transformers work?"

Stored document:

> "Self-attention allows tokens to attend to one another."

Although the words are different,

their embeddings are close in vector space.

Cosine similarity detects this relationship.

This enables **semantic search**, where results are based on meaning rather than exact keyword matches.

---

## Why RAG Uses Cosine Similarity

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) works in three major steps.

1. Convert documents into embeddings.
2. Convert the user's question into an embedding.
3. Retrieve the most similar document embeddings.

The retrieval step depends on a similarity metric.

Cosine similarity is one of the most commonly used choices because it focuses on semantic direction rather than embedding magnitude.

This allows a question to retrieve relevant information even when it shares few or no exact words with the stored documents.

Without cosine similarity (or another suitable similarity metric), RAG would behave much more like traditional keyword search and lose much of its semantic understanding.

---

## Connection to Transformers

Transformers convert text into high-dimensional vectors called **embeddings**.

Every

- word
- sentence
- paragraph
- document

can be represented as a point in a high-dimensional vector space.

The transformer's job is to produce embeddings where similar meanings are located close together.

Cosine similarity is then used to measure how close those embeddings are.

For example,

```
"Artificial Intelligence"

↓

Transformer

↓

Embedding

↓

Cosine Similarity

↓

Nearest Documents
```

Although transformers create the embeddings, cosine similarity is what allows us to compare them effectively.

Together, they form the foundation of semantic search and modern retrieval systems.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The dot product depends on both direction and magnitude.
- Cosine similarity removes the effect of vector magnitude.
- It measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors.
- Values range from **-1** to **1**.
- A value close to **1** indicates strong semantic similarity.
- Sentence embeddings are compared using cosine similarity rather than raw text.
- RAG systems retrieve documents by comparing embedding similarity.
- Vector databases such as **FAISS**, **ChromaDB**, and **Pinecone** use cosine similarity (or closely related distance metrics) for efficient semantic retrieval.
- Transformers generate embeddings, while cosine similarity measures how semantically close those embeddings are.
- Cosine similarity is one of the foundational mathematical tools behind modern Generative AI.
